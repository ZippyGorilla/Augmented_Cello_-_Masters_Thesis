Chapter 4 – Conclusion and Future Work This thesis documents the design and evaluation of a non-contact cello. Participant feedback supported the design goals, reflecting experiences similar to those described by Lydia Kavina on the theremin and Jean-Michel Jarre on the laser harp, while also recognizing the augmented cello as a distinct instrument despite its limitations. The main criticism concerned its limited pitch accuracy. Potential improvements include testing alternative sensors, such as the VL53L1X with a focusing lens, and addressing the IMU bow limitations by switching to an M5 Atom S3R. Chapter 1 showcased state-of-the-art methods to adapt different parts of the cello instrument to match those of a Theremin or laser harp. Chapter 2 made a practical reduction in tactile interaction from these methods, resulting in a design that augments an acoustic cello. The chosen methods for strings were influenced by Woodruff and Görmez’s bachelors thesis: a laser harp with variable pitch along each laser beam [9, p. 5], and a laser harp design by Adafruit Industries [13]. For the bow, IMU use by Andi Otto’s ‘Fello’ [16], and Diana Young’s Hyperbow [17]. The resulting method was almost successful, as lasers were not used. However, they could be used in the future if a rigorously stable mounting system could be designed to hold them steadily from the cello’s peg box. Chapter 3 used an evaluation process, combining autobiographical design [20] with that of a framework by P. J. C. Reimer et al. [21]. Three cellists brought varied perspectives, converging in a celebration of the augmented cello, though a longer trial period and external evaluators, as suggested by Reimer, could bring more beneficial feedback. Further improvements to the augmented cello could include applying Kalman smoothing to enhance the accuracy of VL53L sensor distance readings, or exploring camera-based tracking systems, such as Shir Goldstein’s approach of aligning audio and visual data to transcribe guitar performances into notation [35]. Additional bow control could be developed by drawing from designs by Diana Young and Stefania Serafin, which incorporate pressure sensors [36]. While such sensors could expand expressive control—such as timbre variation—they would reintroduce physical contact, making their inclusion debatable.
